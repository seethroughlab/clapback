🎵 clapback

A public commons of CLAP audio embeddings — 512-dimensional vectors describing what a recording sounds like. Expensive to compute, trivial to compare, so worth computing once and sharing.

Keyed on anonymised SHA256 fingerprint hashes: no titles, no artists, no filenames. Familiar is its first client and largest contributor, and is not its owner — anything can contribute.

How it works

A fingerprint, never a filename

A client fingerprints the audio itself — an acoustic summary, not a tag — hashes that with SHA256, and sends the hash. The corpus never receives a title, an artist, a filename or a path, and cannot recover one.

That is a real limit as much as a privacy property: you can ask about a recording you already have, because you can compute its hash. You cannot yet ask "what does this record I do not own sound like".

One implementation, deliberately

Everyone runs the same code — clapback-embed, published and pinned. The checkpoint alone is not enough: the mel front-end, the windowing rule, the pooling and the precision all move a vector, so all five are composed into one identity string that travels with every contribution.

Two vectors are comparable when that string matches, and not otherwise. Without it, two contributors running different code looks exactly like two contributors disagreeing about the audio, and nothing afterwards can tell them apart.

The first vector wins, the rest are measured

A contribution for a recording already in the corpus does not overwrite it and is not thrown away. It is compared against what is stored and the similarity recorded.

That is how the commons will find out whether independent contributors actually agree. It has not found out yet: almost every vector here came from one library, and a count of submissions is not a count of contributors. The mechanism is in place ahead of the evidence, on purpose.

What is measured is the implementation. The same audio through two CPU architectures agrees to 6.6e-11 — some seven orders of magnitude tighter than two rips of one recording differ from each other. That gap is the room a consensus scheme has to work in.

What it does not claim

The pipeline a contributor declares is asserted, not proven. The server believes it. That catches the forgotten version bump and the stale build, which are the realistic failures; it is not a defence against someone who lies.

Agreement is reported rather than ruled on. No threshold here declares a vector trustworthy — the corpus tells you how many independent submissions matched and how closely, and leaves the judgement to you.
